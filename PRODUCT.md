# Product

## Register

brand

## Platform

web

## Users

The site serves three audiences. The parent reader is the one the clinic was built around and stays first; the other two are where the clinic wants to grow.

**Local families (the parent reader).** Parents in The Gap and the nearby western Brisbane suburbs (Ashgrove, Bardon, Keperra, Ferny Grove, Mitchelton) looking for help for their child, in person. They arrive worried, often comparing a few clinics, often on a phone, and often before they have language for what is going on. The clinic's main focus is children, so the person reading the site is rarely the person who will sit in the room — the site speaks to the parent about their kid's experience.

The job they are trying to get done is to work out whether Therapy House is somewhere their child would be okay, and whether the people here are any good. Credibility is the specific thing that stops them: not price, not location, but whether these are real, capable practitioners. That question has to be answerable without them asking for it.

**The Telehealth Audience.** Adults and families anywhere in Australia who would see a Therapy House psychologist by video, because there is no psychologist near them or the local wait is long. Regional Queensland comes first: Toowoomba, Townsville, Cairns, Rockhampton and the other regional centres, where psychologists are scarce. (Telehealth means a session by video or phone instead of in the clinic's rooms.) They have never seen the house and never will; they need to know quickly that video sessions are a normal service here, that Medicare rebates apply, who they would see, and how to book. City people who prefer video are a side benefit, not the target.

**The Adult Audience.** Adults looking for help for themselves rather than for a child: individual therapy for anxiety, depression, trauma or stress, couples therapy, or an adult ADHD or autism assessment. A site that reads as a children's clinic loses them on the first screen, so the pages written for them speak to the adult directly, and the home page offers them a route of their own. The belief ladder below applies to them with "my child" read as "me".

## Product Purpose

The site carries the clinic's whole story — services, assessments, group programs, fees, team, resources, contact, telehealth — so that a visitor can get everything they need here and the site does the explaining. The house is how you arrive: the exterior turntable is the site map, and each room holds a part of the clinic.

The pages a visitor comes to search for and compare are **plain pages, not rooms**: every therapy and assessment page, the Telehealth page, the Town Pages (one plain page per regional town for the Telehealth Audience, which never implies an office in that town), the fees page, and the privacy policy. They use the plain page layout, load no house experience, and put the answer first. A Town Page or a service page never gets a room or a house door; the house stays optional everywhere.

Success is a visitor finding everything they came for without having to phone to ask a basic question, and arriving at the booking or the call already knowing who they will be dealing with.

## Positioning

Therapy House does not feel clinical. It is a house, not a waiting room — and the website is the first proof of that, not a description of it.

## Conversion & proof

- Primary and secondary CTA: booking online through the Power Diary portal and calling reception on 07 3300 2170 carry **equal** weight. Some parents self-serve, others need to talk first; neither path is the fallback for the other, and neither gets buried.
- The line a visitor remembers after 10 seconds: *"Help that doesn't feel like help."*
- Belief ladder, in order: this place will not feel clinical to my child → these are real, qualified people who know what they are doing → they handle situations like ours → contacting them is easy and low-stakes. Credibility is the rung that carries the most weight; the warmth is what earns the attention to reach it.
- A video session is booked the same way as an in-person one (online or by phone); every page written for the Telehealth Audience says so.
- Proof on hand:
  - **The Medicare Rebate statement**, as the clinic publishes it: "Medicare rebates are available via GP referral and Mental Health Care Plan for eligible individual sessions." Medicare does not cover couple and family sessions, and Medicare and private health insurance cannot be used for the same session. (A Medicare rebate is the part of the fee Medicare pays back to the client.) This is the clinic's main payment path and a fact many visitors search for, so it is stated plainly wherever fees are discussed. The rebate dollar amount is never published unless the clinic confirms it.
  - Media appearances and publications referenced in the library are real and usable.
  - AHPRA registration numbers (AHPRA is the national body that registers psychologists) are still to be supplied by the clinic, and are published only once supplied.
- **No testimonials about clinical care.** Section 133 of the Health Practitioner Regulation National Law bans using testimonials in advertising a regulated health service, and a clinic's website counts as advertising. So client reviews or quotes about therapy or assessments are never used as proof, on the site or in structured data. Proof comes from facts: who the people are, their registration, what the clinic offers, and how Medicare applies.

## Brand Personality

Curious, gentle, hopeful. Storybook wonder rather than instruction: things move, things light up, there is something to find. The voice is unhurried and plain, never brochure-clinical and never talking down.

## Anti-references

- **The psychology-clinic template.** Stock photos of smiling strangers, teal-and-white, sterile card grids, an "Our Services" accordion.
- **The corporate SaaS landing page.** Gradient hero, feature-card triptych, testimonial carousel, "Get started free" energy.
- **The slow, heavy tech demo.** Impressive but exhausting — long loads, unskippable animation, an experience that gets between a worried parent and a phone number.

No specific reference sites; work from the personality and the anti-references rather than chasing a lane.

## Design Principles

**House to arrive, plain to read.** The house is how you enter and how you feel. The moment a visitor is after a specific — an assessment price, a fee, a booking — the content turns clear and scannable and gets product-grade treatment. Atmosphere never taxes the answer.

**Never make a worried parent wait.** Load, motion, and navigation are judged by someone who needs a phone number right now. Anything ambient must be skippable, interruptible, and never on the critical path to information.

**Credibility is carried by people.** The named practitioners, their range and their specialisms are the answer to the question that actually blocks the booking. Team content is load-bearing, not an About page.

**Show the place, don't claim it.** "Warm" and "not clinical" are never asserted in copy. The house, the light, the dog, the hand-drawn rooms do that work; the words stay plain.

**Something to find in every room.** Discovery is the reward for exploring — but it decorates the content, it never hides it.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Solid fundamentals done properly, without committing to an audited standard: full keyboard access, sufficient contrast, meaningful alt text for the illustrated scenes, and honest `prefers-reduced-motion` alternatives for the parallax, firelight and transition video. The clinic serves neurodivergent families, so motion and stimulation deserve more care than the baseline — but no formal WCAG target is being claimed.

We are all no strangers to the practices of journalling and mindfulness. Likely considered some of the most accessible and common forms of selfcare and wellness in today’s age, people all around the world are incorporating these into their lifestyle for a chance to become better versions of themselves and nurture their wellbeing.

## Mindfulness as noticing rather than fixing

Mindfulness encourages people to notice their thoughts, emotions and bodily sensations without immediately judging, suppressing or acting on them. In short, it is the mere art of observing and recognising internal patterns. This can create a small but important **pause between an internal experience and a behavioural response**.

Mindfulness generally involves two key stages:

1. **Awareness:** Placing attention on one’s own present-moment experiences.
2. **Acceptance:** Observing and accepting streams of thought and inner processes, rather than criticising or avoiding.

## Mindfulness and emotional regulation

Regularly practising mindfulness may strengthen someone’s capacity to observe difficult emotions without becoming completely absorbed by them. This allows us to develop the ability to simply observe and accept our internal experiences without suppressing them or trying to change what is happening. Plenty of existing meta-analytic evidence suggests mindfulness-based interventions can improve anxiety and depressive symptoms. 

## Journalling as emotional processing

Putting thoughts and feelings into words can help organise overwhelming experiences, creating some psychological distance from the experience. This makes it easier to digest and process.

Research on expressive writing suggests small but meaningful benefits for depression, anxiety and stress (although effects vary across individuals and approaches). 

## Journalling as a bridge between experience and reflection

Writing can move someone from *“I feel overwhelmed”* towards *“What happened? What am I feeling? What do I need?”*.

Words can be understood as a human’s way of organising experiences. With this in mind, writing can provide a great outlet while also supporting self-awareness and emotional literacy, and allowing more deliberate reflection time rather than automatic reactions to a situation. Writing lets someone observe their thoughts as ‘words on paper’ rather than causing them to become overwhelmed by and merged with their thoughts.

## Keep it flexible

Journalling is not a ‘one-size-fits-all’ activity. It does not need to involve writing pages upon pages about difficult experiences, as people may immediately assume when they think of journalling. Though this approach may work for some, others may find it too time-consuming, challenging, or simply not beneficial.

Brief prompts can be enough to make the practice more accessible and prevent it from becoming another task or opportunity for rumination.

These can include questions like:

- *“What am I noticing?”*
- *“What am I feeling in my body?”*
- *“What do I need right now?”*
- *“What is within my control?”*

For those who do enjoy sitting down and writing for lengthier periods of time about whatever inspires in the moment, you still get to enjoy the benefits of journalling. There’s research suggesting that a ‘free association’ type of writing could be ‘cathartic’; a good way to download our thoughts without any filters or barriers.

## Suggested practices

You may find that incorporating both mindfulness and journalling into one regular practice may be beneficial.

One framework that might be useful is:

***“notice → write → reflect → respond”***

- **Notice –** Mindfulness helps someone notice what is happening internally.
- **Write –** journalling helps them put it into words and identify patterns;
- **Reflect & Respond –** reflection then creates an opportunity to choose a more intentional response.

## Personal implementation

Practising mindfulness and journalling may be a simpler alternative to more expensive and time-intensive habits to fit into your daily routines. It is also sometimes used in therapeutic settings to help people avoid dysfunctional or automatic responses. Next time you need a moment of clarity or self-reflection, try some brief prompts before diving in headfirst. And remember, everyone’s brains are unique – you may need to tailor your practice more towards how you know your own mind to be.

Many women first wonder about ADHD in their twenties, thirties or later, often after years of feeling that everyday organisation is harder for them than for other people. This article describes the signs of ADHD in adult women, explains why ADHD in girls and women is often missed, and sets out how to get assessed in Australia.

ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) is a condition of brain development that begins in early childhood. It is more commonly diagnosed in boys, but healthdirect, the Australian Government's health information service, notes that it is underdiagnosed in girls and in adults.

## Why ADHD is often missed in girls and women

The Australian ADHD clinical practice guideline, written by the Australasian ADHD Professionals Association (AADPA), says ADHD may be under-recognised in girls and women. The reasons it gives are:

- **Inattention is less visible than hyperactivity.** In girls and women, the inattentive signs (trouble focusing, forgetfulness, disorganisation) may be more prominent than the hyperactive and impulsive ones. The hyperactive, restless picture of ADHD is easier to spot in a primary school classroom, so it is more likely to reach a clinician.
- **Parents may not recognise the signs.** The guideline notes that parents may under-recognise hyperactive and impulsive symptoms in girls.
- **Other diagnoses come first.** Girls and women with ADHD may have strong difficulty managing their emotions, and sometimes receive other diagnoses, such as anxiety or depression, instead of ADHD.

The gap between males and females narrows in adulthood. The guideline notes that ADHD may be easier to identify in women during adulthood, because women may refer themselves once they become aware of their symptoms.

## Signs of ADHD in adult women

ADHD has three presentations (patterns of symptoms): mainly inattentive, mainly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined. The inattentive presentation is the most common.

Signs of inattention can include:

- starting tasks but not finishing them
- trouble remembering things, such as appointments or bills
- losing things often
- finding it hard to manage time and stay organised
- putting off tasks or decisions (procrastination).

Signs of hyperactivity and impulsivity can include:

- fidgeting and squirming more than others
- acting without thinking
- interrupting, or blurting out answers before a question is finished
- finding it hard to wait.

Healthdirect also notes that adults with ADHD may change careers often, and may experience depression, anxiety or problems with alcohol or other drugs.

The AADPA guideline adds that ADHD symptoms may change across the menstrual cycle and through reproductive stages such as pregnancy and menopause, although the evidence on this is still limited. A clinician assessing a woman for ADHD should take this into account.

## Signs are not a diagnosis

Everyone loses their keys or misses a deadline sometimes. ADHD is diagnosed only when the symptoms:

- started before the age of 12
- have lasted for more than 6 months
- cause real problems in at least two areas of life, such as home and work
- get in the way of everyday life.

Other things, including anxiety, depression, lack of sleep and some medical conditions, can cause similar difficulties. That is why a proper assessment matters, rather than an online checklist.

## How to get assessed as an adult woman

1. **Talk to your GP.** Your GP can check for other causes and refer you to a psychologist or psychiatrist. In Queensland, specialist GPs can now also start ADHD medicine for adults.
2. **Collect your history.** An adult diagnosis needs evidence that the signs were present in childhood, so old school reports help. A parent, sibling or partner may be asked to fill in a questionnaire.
3. **Have the assessment.** A psychologist or psychiatrist will interview you about your symptoms, history, strengths and daily life, and use standard questionnaires. Because anxiety and depression often come with ADHD in women, a good assessment checks for those too.

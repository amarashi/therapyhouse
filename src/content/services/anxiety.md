Everyone feels anxious sometimes. Anxiety becomes something to get help with
when it is out of proportion to what is happening, when it does not settle,
or when you, or your child, start organising life around avoiding it. That is
the point where a psychologist can help, and anxiety is one of the most
treatable problems people bring to therapy.

## Anxiety in adults

In adults anxiety often looks like worry that will not switch off, trouble
sleeping, a racing heart or tight chest, irritability, and putting things off
because they feel too much. It can come as panic attacks: sudden waves of fear
with a pounding heart, breathlessness or dizziness, which can be frightening
enough that people go to hospital thinking something is physically wrong.
Social anxiety is a strong fear of being judged or embarrassed, which can make
work, study and friendships hard.

## Anxiety in children and teenagers

Children do not always say they are worried. Anxiety can show up as tummy
aches and headaches, tears or anger at drop-off, trouble sleeping, needing a
lot of reassurance, or refusing to do things other children enjoy.

**School avoidance** (sometimes called school refusal) is when a child or
teenager finds it very hard, or impossible, to go to school, often with
physical complaints on school mornings. It is usually driven by anxiety, and
it tends to get harder the longer it goes on, so it is worth getting help
early.

**Separation anxiety** is strong distress about being apart from a parent or
carer, well beyond what is usual for the child's age. Our library article on
[CBT for separation anxiety in children](/library/cbt-for-separation-anxiety-in-children/)
explains what treatment involves and what parents can do at home.

With children, parents are part of the work. The psychologist will usually
meet you first, and will show you how to respond to your child's worry in ways
that build their confidence rather than the anxiety. See
[psychology for children and teenagers](/children/) for how sessions with
children work here.

## OCD (obsessive-compulsive disorder)

OCD is a kind of anxiety problem with two parts. Obsessions are unwanted,
upsetting thoughts, images or urges that keep coming back, such as fear of
germs, of harming someone, or that something is not quite right. Compulsions
are things a person feels driven to do to make the anxiety go away: washing,
checking, counting, arranging, or repeating words in their head. The relief
does not last, so the cycle repeats and can take hours of the day. OCD affects
children and teenagers as well as adults.

The main psychological treatment for OCD is a form of CBT called exposure and
response prevention (ERP). With the psychologist's support, you face the
things that trigger the obsessions in small, planned steps, and practise not
doing the compulsion. Over time the anxiety falls by itself, and the urge to
perform the compulsion weakens. Ask reception which clinician would suit you
or your child.

## How CBT and ACT are used

**CBT (cognitive behaviour therapy)** is the most widely used therapy for
anxiety. It helps you notice the anxious predictions your mind makes ("I will
panic", "something bad will happen"), test them against what actually
happens, and gradually face situations you have been avoiding, so that you
learn that you can cope.

**ACT (acceptance and commitment therapy)** takes a different angle. Instead
of trying to argue with anxious thoughts, you learn to notice them and let
them be there without letting them decide what you do, and to act on what
matters to you.

The two are often combined. More about both is on
[the therapies we use](/therapy/approaches/). Help with anxiety is also one of
the things [adults come to us for](/adults/), and you can read more in our
article [Do you really know what anxiety is?](/library/do-you-really-know-what-anxiety-is/).

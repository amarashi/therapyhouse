One of the most powerful psychological tools for nurturing healthy relationships and resolving conflict is something that, at first glance, seems paradoxical: holding two emotions, or even two seemingly contradictory truths, as valid at the same time.

In moments of interpersonal tension, it’s natural for people to retreat into binary thinking. Someone is right; someone is wrong. One person is hurt; the other is to blame. But human emotions are rarely that clear-cut. In reality, both people in a conflict can be hurting. Both can have valid perspectives. And both can hold emotions that are real even if they seem to contradict one another.

## The Psychology of Emotional Complexity

Psychologically speaking, this concept is rooted in dialectical thinking, the capacity to hold multiple, sometimes opposing ideas or emotions as true simultaneously. This is a central idea in Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), a therapeutic model developed by Dr. Marsha Linehan, which emphasizes the balance between acceptance and change.

Being able to say, “I’m angry with you, and I still love you,” or “I understand your perspective, and I feel hurt by it,” reflects a high level of emotional maturity. It acknowledges that emotional experiences are multi-layered, and that we don’t have to choose one feeling over another in order for our experiences to be valid.

This dual awareness helps to de-escalate conflict. It creates psychological space for empathy and mutual understanding, rather than defensiveness or shutdown. When two people in a relationship can each hold their own feelings as valid and simultaneously respect the validity of the other’s emotions, they shift from adversaries to collaborators.

## Why This Matters in Relationships

In conflict, people often fall into the trap of emotional invalidation – believing that if their feelings are real, the other persons can’t be! But relationships are not zero-sum. Validating someone else’s feelings doesn’t mean agreeing with their behaviour or abandoning your own needs. It simply means recognizing that their emotional response is real for them, shaped by their unique perceptions, history, and internal world.

For example:

- A parent might feel exhausted and frustrated with a child’s tantrum, while also recognising that the child is overwhelmed and scared.
- In a partnership, one person might feel neglected, while the other feels overburdened and emotionally depleted.
- A friend might feel hurt by a cancelled plan, while the other is coping with anxiety and doing the best they can.

Both emotional experiences can be valid, and holding space for both allows for repair, rather than rupture.

## Conflict Resolution Through Validation

When people feel seen and understood, they are less likely to escalate, withdraw, or retaliate. In contrast, emotional invalidation tends to intensify conflict, increase shame, and shut down communication. By acknowledging that two people can feel differently (and that both feelings can be real), you create a foundation for collaborative problem-solving.

This doesn’t mean glossing over pain or bypassing accountability. In fact, holding emotional duality often requires more honesty, not less. It allows someone to say, “I know you didn’t mean to hurt me, and it still did,” or “I see that you’re overwhelmed, and I also need support.”

This nuanced communication builds trust. It helps relationships withstand conflict because it roots them in empathy rather than opposition.

## Practicing Emotional Duality

Here are a few ways to begin embracing this psychological flexibility in daily life:

- Name both feelings. Start with yourself: “I feel grateful for my partner, and I feel lonely sometimes.”
- Avoid ‘but’; use ‘and’. “I understand where you’re coming from, and I still feel disappointed.” The word and creates space for both realities.
- Validate without agreeing. “I see that you’re upset. That makes sense given what happened.”
- Hold complexity during conflict. Slow down and ask, “What’s true for them? What’s true for me? Can both be valid?”

## Final Thoughts

Embracing emotional duality doesn’t make conflict disappear but it changes how we navigate it. It allows for more compassionate conversations, deeper understanding, and stronger connections. In a world that often pushes us to simplify and take sides, learning to hold two truths at once might be the most radical act of empathy we can offer.

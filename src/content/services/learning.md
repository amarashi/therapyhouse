Some children work hard, have help at school and at home, and still fall
behind in reading, writing or maths. A psycho-educational assessment (an
assessment of how a person thinks and learns, and of their reading, writing and
maths skills) helps explain why, and what would help.

It combines two kinds of testing:

- **Cognitive testing** looks at thinking skills: understanding language,
  solving problems, working memory and how quickly your child takes in
  information. Tests the clinic uses include the WISC-V (Wechsler Intelligence
  Scale for Children, for ages 6 to 16) and, for older teenagers and adults,
  the WAIS-IV.
- **Academic testing** looks at reading, spelling, writing and maths, using a
  test such as the WIAT (Wechsler Individual Achievement Test).

Your child's results are compared with a large group of people of the same age
who did the same tasks in the same way. That shows which skills are in the
expected range and which are well above or below it.

## Specific learning disorders

A specific learning disorder is a lasting difficulty learning a particular
academic skill. There are three types. A child can have one, two or all three.

To diagnose one, the psychologist needs to show that your child's skills in that
area are well below what is expected for their age or year level, and that this
is not better explained by something else, such as:

- a general intellectual disability
- missed schooling
- learning in a language that is still new to them
- hearing or vision difficulties

### Dyslexia

Dyslexia is a specific learning disorder in **reading**. It can affect reading
accuracy, reading speed or understanding what has been read. Spelling
difficulties can also be a sign. Signs include:

- reading familiar words by sight but struggling to sound out new ones
- spelling words the way they sound, after spelling rules have been taught
- letter reversals that keep going past the early school years

### Dysgraphia

Dysgraphia is a specific learning disorder in **written expression**. It can
affect grammar and punctuation, and how clearly ideas are expressed and
organised on the page. Signs include:

- plenty of ideas when talking, but great difficulty getting them down in writing
- trouble remembering how to form letters

### Dyscalculia

Dyscalculia is a specific learning disorder in **maths**. It can affect number
sense, calculation and remembering number facts, or mathematical reasoning.
Signs include:

- trouble with simple sums like 2 + 3
- still counting on fingers for basic problems beyond the early primary years
- difficulty with everyday ideas like telling the time, using money or reading a calendar

## Who carries out the assessment

Therapy House has fully registered psychologists and provisional psychologists
(psychologists who are completing the final stage of their training) who are
trained in assessment. Only provisional psychologists with relevant training
take on assessment work, and **every report is signed off by a fully registered
psychologist**.

## How the results are used at school

The report explains your child's strengths and the areas that need support. It
makes recommendations that you can share with the school, such as adjustments
to how your child is taught or how their work is assessed. A diagnosis can also
help your child understand that a subject being hard for them does not mean they
are not clever. Their brain is wired differently, and they can learn other ways
of getting things done.

Learning assessments are also available for teenagers and adults, for example
to support a request for adjustments at university or TAFE.

## What to bring

School information matters most for a learning assessment:

- school reports, including older ones
- NAPLAN results, if your child has sat NAPLAN (the national literacy and numeracy tests in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9)
- notes from any extra help, such as reading intervention, tutoring, occupational therapy or a speech assessment
- any earlier assessment reports
- a referral, if you have one

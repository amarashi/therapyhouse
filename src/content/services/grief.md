Grief is the natural response to losing someone or something that mattered.
It is not an illness, and there is no right way or right length of time to
grieve. Counselling can help when grief feels too heavy to carry alone, when
it seems stuck, or when it is mixed up with other things: guilt, anger, a
difficult relationship with the person who died, or the shock of a sudden
loss.

## What grief can look like

Grief is usually thought of as sadness, but it can include many feelings,
sometimes all in one day: shock, numbness, anger, guilt, relief, longing and
fear. It can also be physical, with tiredness, poor sleep, changes in appetite
and trouble concentrating.

Grief does not only follow a death. People grieve a separation or divorce, a
miscarriage or a pregnancy that did not happen, a diagnosis, the loss of a
job, a move away from home and culture, or the slow loss of someone to
dementia or illness.

For most people the pain becomes more bearable over time, even if the loss is
never forgotten. For some, intense grief stays for a long time and makes it
hard to get on with life. That is a good reason to talk to someone, and so is
grief that has turned into a low mood that will not lift; see
[depression counselling](/therapy/depression/).

## Children and grief

Children grieve too, but they show it differently from adults. A young child
may seem fine one minute and be crying the next, may ask the same questions
again and again, or may go back to behaviour they had grown out of, such as
bed-wetting or clinging. Older children and teenagers may withdraw, be angry,
struggle at school, or try to look after everyone else.

What helps most is honest, simple information in words that suit their age,
keeping ordinary routines going, and letting them ask questions and show
their feelings. If your child is struggling, a psychologist can see them, and
can also help you work out how to talk with them about what has happened. See
[psychology for children and teenagers](/children/) for how sessions with
children work here.

## How counselling helps

Grief counselling is not about moving on or forgetting. It gives you time and
space to talk about the person or the thing you have lost, to make sense of
what happened, and to find a way to carry the loss alongside the rest of your
life. Sessions may cover:

- the feelings you have not been able to share elsewhere, including the ones
  that feel unacceptable, such as anger or relief;
- practical things that have become hard, such as sleep, work and anniversaries;
- unfinished business, regrets, or a difficult relationship with the person
  who died;
- how to stay connected to the person in a way that feels right to you.

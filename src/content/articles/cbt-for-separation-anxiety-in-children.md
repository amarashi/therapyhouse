Most young children get upset when a parent leaves. For some, the worry is so strong and lasts so long that it gets in the way of school, sleep and family life. This article explains how cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for separation anxiety works in children, how to tell ordinary clinginess from separation anxiety disorder, and what parents can do at home.

## Normal worry or separation anxiety disorder?

It is normal for young children to be upset when they are away from their parents or carers. Most grow out of it as they learn that the people they love come back, and that they are safe while they are apart.

Separation anxiety disorder is different. Healthdirect, the Australian Government's health information service, lists these signs:

- intense distress when separated from loved ones or away from home, or even when thinking about it
- taking longer than expected to settle after a separation
- worrying that something bad will happen to loved ones while apart
- avoiding time apart, such as refusing to sleep away from home or away from a parent
- nightmares about separation, and panic at the moment of separation
- clinginess, crying, tantrums, headaches or tummy aches when it is time to separate.

The Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne adds that it can show up as school refusal.

For a diagnosis in a child, the symptoms must have lasted at least 4 weeks, be more severe than expected for the child's age, cause significant distress, and interfere with everyday life. Separation anxiety is one of the most common anxiety disorders in children.

## What is CBT?

Cognitive behavioural therapy, or CBT, is a talking therapy that looks at how a person's thoughts, feelings and actions are connected. It helps people notice and challenge unhelpful thinking patterns, and change what they do in response.

Healthdirect notes that anxiety disorders in children can often be managed with CBT, face to face or online, and that therapy can be with the child alone, in a group, or with family members. The Royal Children's Hospital's clinical guideline recommends psychological therapy as the first treatment for anxiety in children in most situations, and recommends CBT for children aged 8 to 18 with mild to moderate symptoms. Medicine is rarely recommended for anxiety in children, and is not the first choice.

## How CBT helps with separation anxiety

CBT for a child is adjusted to their age. In general terms, it can help a child and family to:

- **Understand anxiety.** Learning what anxiety is, and how it can show up in the body (such as tummy aches or headaches), is the starting point. Both the child and the parents learn this.
- **Notice worried thoughts.** For example, "Something bad will happen to Mum while I'm at school." The child learns to notice these thoughts and challenge them.
- **Face separations step by step.** Rather than avoiding separations, the child practises them in small, manageable steps, building up slowly.
- **Learn ways to cope.** Calm breathing or a reassuring phrase the child can say to themselves help in the moment.
- **Involve parents.** The Royal Children's Hospital notes that treatments for anxiety in children often involve parents or carers. Parents can then support the same steps at home.

For younger children, especially those under 8, the Royal Children's Hospital guideline notes that play therapy may be considered instead.

## What parents can do at home

Australian health services suggest these steps while you wait for help, or alongside therapy:

- Acknowledge your child's fears. Do not dismiss or ignore them.
- Gently encourage your child to do the things they are anxious about, taking it slowly, and praise their effort.
- Try not to let your child avoid the things that make them anxious. Avoidance can turn into a habit that is hard to break.
- Make a plan for anxious moments together, such as breathing slowly or using a reassuring phrase.
- Keep a healthy routine with enough sleep, outdoor exercise, regular meals and limited screen time.
- Avoid labelling your child as "shy" or "anxious".
- Talk to your child's teacher or school counsellor.

## When to get help

See your GP if the anxiety does not go away or get better with time, gets worse, or affects your child's daily life, schooling or friendships. Your GP can check your child's health, write a mental health treatment plan, and refer you to a psychologist. A mental health treatment plan gives a Medicare rebate for up to 10 individual sessions a year.

Cognitive skills are the thinking skills that learning is built on: memory,
understanding language, solving problems, planning and making decisions. A
cognitive assessment measures them, so you can see what your child does
easily, what they find harder, and how that affects them at school and at home.

## What a cognitive assessment measures

Your child completes a range of activities with the psychologist. They look at
four main areas:

- **Verbal comprehension:** understanding and using words and ideas
- **Problem solving:** working things out, including with shapes and patterns
- **Working memory:** holding information in mind while using it
- **Processing speed:** how quickly your child takes in information and responds

The tests are standardised. That means every child does the same tasks in the
same way, and your child's results are compared with a large group of children
of the same age. The report shows which skills sit in the average range and
which are well above or below it. Tests the clinic uses include the WISC-V
(Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, for ages 6 to 16) and the UNIT-2
(Universal Nonverbal Intelligence Test, which does not rely on spoken language).

A cognitive assessment can help:

- explain difficulties your child is having
- show strengths your child can use when choosing what and how to learn
- guide adjustments at school and strategies at home
- show where extra help would make the most difference, and early help tends to work best
- inform other assessments, such as for a learning disorder, intellectual disability, autism or ADHD

## Giftedness

Some children seem bored in class, say school is pointless because they already
know it all, or bring home excellent reports while losing interest. If you think
your child may be intellectually gifted, a cognitive assessment can show how
their abilities compare with other children their age, so you and the school
can look at more advanced or more stimulating opportunities.

The clinician will talk with you at intake about whether a cognitive
assessment answers your question, or whether more is needed.

## School readiness

Starting school is a big step, and many parents wonder whether their child is
ready, especially a child who will be among the youngest in their year. School
readiness covers social, emotional, language and motor skills as well as
thinking skills. Our article on
[understanding and supporting school readiness](/library/school-readiness/)
sets out the milestones to look for at four and five.

If your child has not met developmental milestones, or a doctor or the kindy has
suggested an assessment, a cognitive assessment can be part of the picture. The
tests used depend on your child's age, so tell reception how old your child is
when you call.

## Other reasons parents ask for one

- your child needs more individual support at school than expected
- your child has trouble following instructions
- your child is struggling to keep up, or their behaviour at school has changed
- your child seems very strong in some areas and much weaker in others

## Who carries out the assessment

Therapy House has fully registered psychologists and provisional psychologists
(psychologists who are completing the final stage of their training) who are
trained in assessment. Only provisional psychologists with relevant training
take on assessment work, and **every report is signed off by a fully registered
psychologist**.

## Learning assessment or cognitive assessment?

A cognitive assessment looks at thinking skills only. If the worry is reading,
writing or maths, the
[comprehensive psycho-educational assessment](/assessments/learning/) adds
academic testing and can diagnose dyslexia, dysgraphia and dyscalculia.

Cognitive assessments are also available for adults, for example to plan
adjustments at university or TAFE. Ask reception for a quote. For other support
for your child, see
[psychology for children and teenagers](/children/).

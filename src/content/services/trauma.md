Trauma therapy is for people who are still affected by something frightening
or overwhelming: an accident, an assault, a sudden death, a birth that went
wrong, or harm that went on for months or years. You do not have to have a
diagnosis, and you do not have to tell the whole story in the first session.

## What trauma and PTSD can look like

After a traumatic event most people are shaken for a while, and many recover
over the following weeks. For some, the effects stay. PTSD (post-traumatic
stress disorder) is the name for a group of reactions that last longer than a
month and get in the way of daily life. They include:

- reliving the event, through unwanted memories, nightmares or flashbacks
  (moments when it feels as if it is happening again);
- avoiding people, places or conversations that bring it back;
- feeling on edge, jumpy, irritable, or unable to sleep or concentrate;
- feeling numb, guilty, ashamed, or cut off from other people.

You can be struggling after trauma without meeting every part of that
description. What matters is whether it is affecting your life.

## How therapy helps

Trauma therapy does not erase what happened. It helps your mind file the
memory as something that is over, so that it stops arriving as if it were
happening now. The work goes at a pace you can manage. The first sessions are
usually about understanding your reactions and learning ways to steady
yourself when they come, before any memory work begins.

The two therapies with the strongest research support for PTSD are EMDR and
trauma-focused CBT, according to the Australian PTSD guidelines published by
Phoenix Australia. You can read about both, and the other therapies used here,
on [the therapies we use](/therapy/approaches/).

## EMDR, explained plainly

EMDR stands for eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing. In an EMDR
session you bring a troubling memory to mind for short periods while your
attention is also on something else, usually your eyes following the
therapist's hand from side to side, or alternating taps or sounds. Between
these short sets you notice what comes up and say it briefly. Over a number of
sessions the memory usually becomes less vivid and less upsetting, and the
beliefs attached to it ("it was my fault", "I am not safe") change too.

You do not have to describe the memory in detail for EMDR to work, which some
people find easier than talking it through. EMDR follows set phases, and
preparation comes first: your therapist makes sure you have ways to calm
yourself before any memory is processed.

**Can EMDR be done by video?** EMDR can be adapted for video sessions, with
taps or on-screen movements in place of a hand in the room. Whether it is the
right choice for you, and whether your clinician offers it by video, is
something to ask reception when you book.

## Trauma-focused CBT

CBT (cognitive behaviour therapy) looks at the links between thoughts,
feelings and actions. Trauma-focused CBT applies that to what happened:
understanding how the event changed the way you see yourself and the world,
gradually and safely facing memories and reminders you have been avoiding, and
practising skills for the anxiety that comes with them.

## Complex trauma

Some trauma is not a single event. Complex trauma is the effect of harm that
was repeated or went on for a long time, often in childhood or within a close
relationship, including family violence. It can shape how safe you feel with
other people, how you handle strong emotions and how you see yourself. Therapy
for complex trauma usually takes longer, and steadiness and safety in the
present come before work on the past.

## Your first session

The first session is a conversation. Your psychologist asks what brings you
in, how it is affecting you now and what you would like to be different. You
decide how much to say about what happened. By the end you should have a
sense of how therapy would work for you, and you can ask any questions.

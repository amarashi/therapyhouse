ADHD and autism are often mentioned together, and some of their signs look alike. This article explains the difference between ADHD and autism, where they overlap, how often a person has both, and how each one is assessed in Australia.

Both are neurodevelopmental conditions, which means they come from differences in how the brain develops. Both begin in childhood, and both can be diagnosed for the first time in teenagers and adults.

## What is ADHD?

ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) mainly affects attention, activity levels and impulse control. It has three presentations (patterns of symptoms):

- **Inattentive:** trouble focusing, being forgetful or disorganised, starting tasks but not finishing them, losing things.
- **Hyperactive-impulsive:** fidgeting and squirming more than others, acting without thinking, interrupting, finding it hard to wait.
- **Combined:** a mix of both.

## What is autism?

Autism (autism spectrum disorder) is a lifelong condition that affects how a person communicates, relates to others and experiences the world. "Spectrum" means it affects people differently. Its signs fall into two broad areas:

- **Social communication and interaction:** for example, finding social conversation difficult, or taking words and ideas literally.
- **Limited and repetitive behaviours and interests:** for example, repeating the same actions, or having a small number of very strong interests.

Many autistic people also react strongly to sensory input, such as loud sounds, bright lights or crowded places.

## The main difference

Put simply, the two conditions are defined by different core difficulties:

- **ADHD** is defined by inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity: how a person regulates their attention, activity and impulses.
- **Autism** is defined by differences in social communication and interaction, together with limited and repetitive behaviours and interests.

The same behaviour can have a different cause in each. A child who does not join in with others at lunchtime might be distracted and moving from one thing to the next, or might find the social side of the game confusing or the noise overwhelming. This is why an assessment looks at why something is happening, not just what is happening.

## Where they overlap

Healthdirect, the Australian Government's health information service, notes that some features of autism overlap with ADHD, so a proper assessment is important. Both conditions:

- begin in early childhood, and can be diagnosed for the first time later in life
- are diagnosed more often in males, and are thought to be underdiagnosed in females. Healthdirect notes that many autistic women find out they are autistic in adulthood, and that ADHD is underdiagnosed in girls and in adults
- are assessed by paediatricians, psychiatrists and psychologists, using interviews and standard questionnaires.

## Can you have both?

Yes. The two often occur together. The Australian ADHD clinical practice guideline, written by the Australasian ADHD Professionals Association (AADPA), reports that autism occurs alongside ADHD in 20 to 50% of cases. Healthdirect lists autism among the conditions that many people with ADHD also have, together with anxiety, depression and learning difficulties.

## How each is assessed in Australia

The usual first step for both is a visit to the GP, who can refer you to a specialist.

**ADHD.** Children are usually assessed by a paediatrician (a doctor who specialises in children's health), a child psychiatrist or a psychologist; adults by a psychiatrist or psychologist. The assessment is a detailed interview about symptoms and history, plus questionnaires completed by the person and by people who know them, such as parents, partners or teachers. Symptoms must have started before age 12.

**Autism.** A specialist uses standardised tools, such as questionnaires and checklists, and this may take several appointments. A paediatrician may also check for physical causes, and a psychiatrist or psychologist makes the diagnosis. Adults are diagnosed by a psychiatrist or psychologist. An autism diagnosis includes a support level from 1 to 3, which describes how much support the person needs.

Because the two overlap and often occur together, a good assessment for one should consider the other.
